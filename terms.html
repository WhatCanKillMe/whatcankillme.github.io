1. What this app is

What Can Kill Me shows two kinds of information: hazards reported by members of the public, and data published by government and scientific organisations.

By using it you agree to these terms. If you do not agree, do not use it.

2. What this app is not — read this part

This is not an emergency service. It cannot summon help. It does not know you are in trouble. Nobody is monitoring it on your behalf. In an emergency, call 911, or use VHF Channel 16 at sea.

It is not a substitute for judgement, training, or preparation. It is one input among many — the same status as a trail report or a conversation in a car park, not a survey of what is actually out there.

Absence of a warning means nothing. If the map shows no hazards in an area, that means nobody has reported one and no agency has published one. It is not evidence that the area is safe. Most of the backcountry has never had a hazard reported in it. This is the single most important thing to understand about this app, and every screen in it is built around saying so.

Reports come from members of the public. They may be wrong, stale, mislocated, exaggerated, or already resolved. A trust score is a rough signal about a contributor, not a verification of a report.

Third-party data may be delayed, incomplete or unavailable. Satellite fire detections are heat signatures, not confirmed fires. Weather alerts cover zones, not points. Species sightings record where somebody stood with a camera. Avalanche forecasts expire. Daylight times describe open sky and will be optimistic under tree cover or in a gorge.

Do not make a life-safety decision on the strength of this app alone.
