2. What this app is

What Can Kill Me shows two kinds of information: hazards reported by members of the public, and data published by government and scientific organizations. It also lets you send a check-in message to people you choose, from your own phone, and lets you save places and areas to watch.

3. What this app is not — read this part

This is not an emergency service. It cannot summon help. It does not know you are in trouble. Nobody is monitoring it on your behalf. In an emergency, call 911 in the United States, your local emergency number elsewhere, or use VHF Channel 16 at sea.

It is not a substitute for judgment, training, or preparation. It is one input among many — the same status as a trail report or a conversation in a parking lot, not a survey of what is actually out there.

Absence of a warning means nothing. If the map shows no hazards in an area, that means nobody has reported one and no agency has published one. It is not evidence that the area is safe. Most of the backcountry has never had a hazard reported in it. This is the single most important thing to understand about this app, and every screen in it is built around saying so.

Reports come from members of the public. They may be wrong, stale, mislocated, exaggerated, or already resolved. A trust score is a rough signal about a contributor, not a verification of a report. A confirmation means another user tapped a button, nothing more.

Third-party data may be delayed, incomplete, or unavailable. The app explains the limits of each source under Map Data, and those explanations are part of these Terms.

Assume the app will not be there when you are out of signal, and plan accordingly. Offline copies are only as current as the last time the app had a connection.

Do not make a life-safety decision on the strength of this app alone.
